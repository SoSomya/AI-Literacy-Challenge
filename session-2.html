Summary

This session focuses on the wider effects of AI. We will look at questions around privacy, data sharing, bias, environmental impact, responsibility and trust. We will also discuss where and why AI may not always be the right tool, and why human judgement is still needed when using AI systems.

You will reflect on the topics taught from this talk and think about how they might show up in your mini challenge and personal projects.

Recap of Session 2

In Session 2, we continued with the foundations of AI by looking at what models are, predictive and generative AI, and a gentle introduction to ethics around AI, particularly the pipeline of production from materials to product.

We compared predictive AI and generative AI. We looked at how machine-learning systems can use examples to predict a category or outcome (like the drawing based neural network game), while generative AI systems can produce text, images, sound, code, and other content (like the example of an image produced from a simple prompt - Capybara with a lotus flower on its head).

We introduced the idea of models and representations. A model is a simplified representation that uses selected information or features to help describe something, recognise patterns, or make predictions. We considered how the choice and quality of data can influence what a system learns and whether its outputs are useful, incomplete, or biased.

We discussed what is known and not known about the development of large generative-AI systems. Training may involve large amounts of text, images, sounds, software code, and other material, much of it collected from the internet. However, there is often limited transparency about the exact training data and about what happens inside complex AI systems, which are sometimes described as a black box.

We also examined the limitations and wider impacts of AI tools, including usage limits, free and paid services, errors, misattributions, human labour, computing infrastructure, and environmental resources. You can revisit the resource from Kate Crawford and Vladan Joler Anatomy of AI System (opens in a new tab).

We discussed using AI as an assistant, producer, or trainer, and why these different uses require different levels of care. AI-generated information can appear convincing while still being wrong, so people remain responsible for checking important claims, reviewing outputs, and deciding whether a tool is appropriate for the task.

Main ideas from the session

  • Predictive AI and generative AI perform different types of tasks.
  • Predictive systems use patterns from examples or data to estimate a category, result, or likely outcome.
  • Generative AI can produce text, images, sound, code, and other content in response to an input or prompt.
  • A model is a simplified representation of a complex system that uses selected information or features to help recognise patterns or make predictions. We used the example of capturing Edinburgh in a model and also looked at how an equation from Isaac Newton could seen as a model of how mass, force and acceleration work together.
  • The choice, quality, and range of training data can influence what an AI system learns and produces.
  • There is often limited transparency about the exact data used to train large generative-AI systems from the current services we have available from companies.
  • Complex AI systems are sometimes described as a black box because it can be difficult to explain fully how an input led to a particular output.
  • AI tools are products supported by computing infrastructure, human labour, organisations, and material resources.
  • AI-generated information may contain errors, misattributions, or misleading claims, even when it appears confident. However, the models are fast improving, and with connections to web search, file reading, and image interpretations, the responses are much improved.
  • People remain responsible for checking outputs, protecting private information, and deciding whether an AI tool is appropriate for a task. How we utilise any given tool, software or device remains within our agency.
  • Using AI as an assistant, producer, or trainer creates different risks and responsibilities.
  • Communities and people affected by AI-supported decisions should have opportunities to participate, ask questions, and raise concerns.

The description contains enough information to support the classification or conclusion.

Statement: A system predicts whether a drawing is a cat, bicycle or tree using patterns learned from many labelled drawings.

Where should this card go?

Look for information about examples, learned patterns and prediction.

Why: The description tells us that the system learned patterns from examples and uses those patterns to predict a category. This supports identifying it as a predictive machine-learning system.

Statement: A website says it uses AI to recommend an activity to each visitor.

Where should this card go?

The phrase “uses AI” does not explain how the recommendation was chosen.

Why: The recommendation might come from fixed rules, a prepared list, a predictive model or another process. We need more information about how the result was produced.

Statement: A chatbot states that a particular person wrote a book about artificial intelligence.

Where should this card go?

Consider whether a confident statement is enough evidence for a factual claim.

Why: The author's name is a factual claim. It should be checked using a suitable source such as the publisher, a library catalogue or the author's official page.

Statement: An AI-generated response includes a link beneath one of its statements.

Where should this card go?

A link may exist without supporting the statement placed beside it.

Why: The link should be opened and compared with the claim. Its presence does not prove that the generated summary is accurate or that the source supports it.

Statement: A company says that its generative-AI model was trained using material collected from the internet.

Where should this card go?

“Material from the internet” describes a very large and unclear collection.

Why: This does not identify the exact sources, permissions, dates, countries, languages or types of material used. More information would be needed to understand the training data.

Statement: A tool creates a new image after a person submits a written description.

Where should this card go?

Look at whether the system is producing new content from an input.

Why: The description supports identifying this as generative AI because the tool produces new image content from a written prompt. Whether the image is suitable or accurate remains a separate question.

Key vocabulary from this session

Click a card to reveal a short definition. These words will help with thinking about safe, ethical, and responsible AI use.

Privacy

Click to reveal definition.

Privacy

Protecting personal, and identifying information and being careful about what is shared, stored, or uploaded about us in digital spaces. Privacy extends beyond information, it includes your physical spaces, and life. The UK has a number of laws around privacy of your data specifically, how it is held, your rights to it and how you might access data held about you, two main laws include GDPR, and The Data Protection Act.

Source: Adapted text description by class tutor. Official link -> UK Gov

Consent

Click to reveal definition.

Consent

Clear permission for something to happen. Consent applies to many contexts, including digital spaces, like consent to use your personal data or data related to you.

Source: Class tutor.

Bias

Click to reveal definition.

Bias

When a system, person, or dataset gives unfair weight to one group, idea, or outcome. Bias has many definitions depending on the context, it can be intentional or unintentional. In terms of AI or machine learning bias, these can include technical elements like the type of data used, how wide ranging data is to capture a complex system, or how a model might be tailored towards specific outputs which create results that can hide or leave out important information. An example might be a model trained on health data from male persons only, and then used to understand health issues for all gender identities. Due to the bias in training data or even an a flaw in the model design, the outputs may not capture health intricacies for female patients.

Source: Class tutor.

Hallucination

Click to reveal definition.

Hallucination

Hallucination is the case when an AI tool gives an answer that sounds confident but is wrong, made up, or unsupported. This has been an issue many some AI models, but newer adaptations and ongoing work is looking to address this phenomenon.

Source: Class tutor.

Verification

Click to reveal definition.

Verification

Verification generally means to check things. CIn the context of this course it would refer to checking information against trusted sources before using it, sharing it, or acting on it.

Source: Class tutor.

Models

Click to reveal definition.

Models

Models typically capture a complex system. They can range from scientific, to mathematical or even physical. For example a scientist might use a 'model' of disease this means they might use a small scale setup that reflect the characteristics of the disease in order to study it, it can be a physical dish of cells or a on a computer based on all of the information we have around that disease and the important features of that disease which help to distinguish it. A mathematical model might capture for instance river activity like water levels based on data collected over time, a researcher might look at flow, estimated movement/quantities and so forth. Alternatively a city planner might design a digital or physical model of a plan for a city centre. The idea of models is best capture a complex system so that it represents that system closely. Machine learning models will be created differently to mathematical models despite requiring mathematics. That is because machine learning models would typically include a training/learning phase on data provided to identify patterns, a mathematical model typically has pre-set formulas set by the expert.

Source: Class tutor
